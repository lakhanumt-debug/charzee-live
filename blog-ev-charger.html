AC vs DC EV Chargers — Which One Do You Actually Need?

The type of charger you choose has a direct impact on charging speed, installation cost and where it can be used. Here is a clear breakdown of how each works.

June 2025 7 min read EV Charging

When people start looking at EV charging infrastructure — for a home, a workplace, a fleet or a public station — one of the first decisions they face is choosing between an AC charger and a DC charger. These are not simply different power levels of the same product; they work in fundamentally different ways and are suited to different situations. Understanding how each works makes the right choice much clearer.

How EV Charging Actually Works

Every electric vehicle has a battery that stores electricity as direct current (DC). However, the electricity available from the grid — in homes, buildings and most power infrastructure — is alternating current (AC). To charge the battery, the power must be converted from AC to DC at some point in the process.

The critical difference between an AC charger and a DC charger is where that conversion happens.

AC Charging — The Charger Is Inside the Vehicle

In AC charging, the charging unit supplies alternating current directly to the vehicle. The conversion from AC to DC happens inside the car, in a component called the onboard charger (OBC). The onboard charger has a fixed maximum capacity — typically between 3.3 kW and 22 kW depending on the vehicle — and this capacity determines the maximum charging speed the vehicle can accept from an AC source.

This means that connecting a 22 kW AC charger to an EV with a 7.2 kW onboard charger will still only charge the vehicle at 7.2 kW. The car itself is the bottleneck, not the charging unit.

Where AC Charging Is Used

  • Homes and apartments for overnight charging
  • Workplaces where vehicles are parked for several hours
  • Hotels, restaurants and retail locations where customers stay for extended periods
  • Destination charging points where speed is not the primary requirement

For most Indian homes, a 7.2 kW AC charger is the practical choice — it works on a standard single-phase connection and can fully charge most mainstream EVs overnight without requiring any special electrical upgrades.

DC Charging — The Conversion Happens Outside the Vehicle

In DC fast charging, the conversion from AC to DC happens inside the charging unit itself. The charger delivers DC power directly to the vehicle's battery, bypassing the onboard charger entirely. This allows the charging rate to be determined by the charging station's output capacity and the battery management system's acceptance rate — rather than the vehicle's onboard charger limit.

Because the conversion hardware is built into the charging station rather than the car, DC chargers are significantly larger, heavier and more expensive than AC units. They also require a considerably larger power supply and a dedicated electrical connection.

Where DC Charging Is Used

  • Highway charging corridors where drivers need a fast top-up and move on
  • Public charging stations in high-traffic commercial locations
  • Fleet depots requiring fast overnight turnaround of multiple vehicles
  • Petrol-station-style fast charging locations

Connector Types in India

Alongside the AC/DC distinction, connectors matter. India has standardised on two connector types for most EVs:

  • Type 2 (Mennekes) — the standard connector for AC charging across passenger EVs in India. Supports up to 22 kW single or three-phase.
  • CCS2 (Combined Charging System) — used for DC fast charging on most passenger EVs sold in India. Supports high-power DC delivery alongside AC capability in a single port.
  • CHAdeMO — used on a smaller number of older EV models. Less common in new vehicles being sold in India today.
  • GB/T — used on some electric buses and commercial vehicles of Chinese origin.

Which Charger Type Do You Need?

The honest answer is: it depends on how long your vehicle will be parked and how quickly it needs to be ready again.

If your EV is parked for 6–10 hours at home every night, an AC charger is almost always the right choice. It is simpler, cheaper to install and easily delivers a full charge within the parking window. There is no practical benefit to a DC fast charger at a location where the vehicle is already parked for hours.

If you are building a public charging station, a highway stop, or a fleet operation where vehicles need to be back in service quickly, DC fast chargers make the economics work — shorter charging times mean more vehicles served per day.

A common mistake is over-specifying. Installing a 60 kW DC fast charger for a parking lot where cars sit for four hours adds significant cost without a corresponding benefit. Matching the charger type to the actual use pattern is what creates a practical, cost-effective installation.
