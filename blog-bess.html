Battery Storage

What Is BESS and Why Does It Matter for India's Energy Future?

Battery Energy Storage Systems are becoming essential infrastructure for reliable power — from homes and factories to EV charging stations and utility grids.

June 2025 6 min read Battery Storage

India's electricity grid faces two persistent challenges: supply is not always available when demand is highest, and an increasing share of power now comes from solar and wind sources that only generate when the sun shines or the wind blows. Battery Energy Storage Systems — commonly referred to as BESS — address both of these problems directly. They store electricity when it is available and release it precisely when it is needed.

What Is a Battery Energy Storage System?

A Battery Energy Storage System is a technology that captures electrical energy and stores it in rechargeable battery cells for later use. At its core, a BESS consists of battery modules (the storage medium), a Battery Management System (BMS) that monitors and protects the cells, a Power Conversion System (PCS) that handles the conversion between DC stored power and AC grid power, and a control system that manages when to charge and discharge based on demand, pricing or grid signals.

BESS installations range from small units designed for a single home or commercial building — typically between 5 kWh and 100 kWh — to large utility-scale systems storing hundreds of megawatt-hours of energy to support entire sections of the electricity grid.

In simple terms: A BESS works like a very large rechargeable battery. It charges when electricity is cheap or plentiful, and discharges when power is expensive, scarce or the grid is unavailable.

How Does BESS Work?

During periods of surplus power — when solar panels are generating more than current consumption requires, or when grid electricity is at a low tariff — the BESS charges. The Battery Management System continuously monitors cell voltage, temperature and state of charge to keep the batteries operating safely and efficiently.

When demand rises — in the evening when solar generation stops, during peak demand hours when grid tariffs are highest, or when a power cut occurs — the system discharges, supplying power to the connected loads. The transition between grid and battery supply is typically seamless, happening in milliseconds.

Advanced BESS installations are also capable of providing grid services such as frequency regulation — responding to small imbalances in the grid to help maintain stable power quality across a wider area.

Battery Technologies Used in BESS

Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP)

LFP is currently the most widely used battery chemistry for stationary BESS installations. It offers a strong combination of safety, thermal stability, long cycle life (typically 4,000–6,000 charge-discharge cycles) and reasonable cost. LFP batteries do not contain cobalt or nickel, making them more stable at high temperatures than some other lithium chemistries — an important consideration for hot Indian climates.

Lithium NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt)

NMC batteries offer higher energy density than LFP, meaning more energy can be stored in a smaller physical footprint. They are used where space is a significant constraint. However, they require more sophisticated thermal management and tend to have shorter cycle life compared to LFP.

Lead-Acid

Traditional lead-acid batteries are still used in smaller, cost-sensitive applications. They are significantly heavier and bulkier than lithium options for the same energy capacity, have shorter cycle life and require more maintenance. For most modern BESS deployments, lithium chemistries have largely replaced lead-acid at all but the smallest scales.

Where Is BESS Used?

Commercial and Industrial Buildings

Factories, data centres, hospitals and large commercial buildings use BESS to manage electricity costs through peak shaving — drawing from stored battery power during expensive peak tariff hours rather than from the grid. This can substantially reduce monthly electricity bills for high-consumption facilities.

Solar Energy Systems

Pairing a BESS with a solar installation addresses the fundamental limitation of solar power: it generates electricity only during daylight hours. Battery storage retains surplus daytime generation and makes it available in the evening and at night, increasing the proportion of total electricity consumption covered by solar from around 30–40% to 70–90% in well-designed systems.

EV Charging Stations

DC fast chargers draw large amounts of power in short bursts. For a charging station, this creates high peak demand charges on the electricity bill and can require expensive grid connection upgrades. A BESS at a charging station stores energy steadily from the grid, then releases it rapidly when a vehicle connects — reducing peak demand charges and lowering the grid connection capacity required.

Residential Backup Power

Home battery systems provide backup during power cuts. When the grid fails, the BESS automatically supplies the home's essential loads — lights, fans, refrigerator, medical equipment — maintaining power continuity without a generator.

Utility Grid Support

At the largest scale, grid-connected BESS installations store excess renewable generation during high-production periods and release it during evening demand peaks. India has been deploying large-scale BESS as part of its strategy to integrate growing solar and wind generation into the national grid reliably.

Key Benefits of BESS

  • Energy independence: Reduces reliance on the grid, particularly during outages or peak tariff periods.
  • Lower electricity costs: Peak shaving and time-of-use optimization cut bills for commercial and industrial users.
  • Better use of solar: Stores surplus solar generation instead of exporting it at low rates or wasting it.
  • Power quality improvement: Stabilises voltage and frequency for sensitive equipment.
  • Reduced infrastructure costs: Can lower required grid connection capacity for high-demand applications like fast charging.
  • Scalability: Systems can be expanded by adding battery modules as requirements grow.

BESS and EV Charging — Why They Work Well Together

One of the most practical pairings in clean energy infrastructure today is a BESS combined with EV charging. The challenge with DC fast charging in particular is its demand profile: a single 120 kW charger draws as much power as 40–50 typical homes when a vehicle is connected. For a charging hub with multiple fast chargers, the peak demand can be enormous — and grid charges are typically calculated on peak demand rather than total consumption.

A BESS installed alongside the charging station charges steadily from the grid at a lower rate, then delivers power quickly when vehicles arrive. The grid sees a smooth, moderate load rather than sharp spikes. The charging station owner pays significantly lower demand charges, and the system can continue operating even during brief grid disturbances.

For solar-powered charging stations, BESS makes the combination genuinely viable around the clock — not just during the hours when the sun is generating power.

As India's EV adoption accelerates and solar capacity continues to grow, BESS is becoming a foundational technology — connecting generation, storage and consumption into a more efficient, resilient energy system.
